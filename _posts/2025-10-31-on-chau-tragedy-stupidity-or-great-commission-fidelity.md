I may be seven years late, but I have to write about this. I have not written in a while, but it appears this is the subject that has caused me to break the silence and write again. My decision to write an entry on John Allen Chau is rooted in the fact that this blog is intended to be my thought space. And John’s story has been swirling in my mind for a while now. It all started when I watched the documentary, entitled _The Mission_, just over a year ago. I had heard bits and pieces of the story from coworkers and fellow believers, but I had not read, watched, or studied anything on Chau myself. So, I watched the National Geographic documentary. I did not really think about Chau much after that — that is, until recently when he came up in a conversation my buddies and I were having. This led me to watch the documentary again, and I have thought about Chau almost every day since. This past weekend, some friends and I went to the movie theatre to see a new film that Hollywood put out about John Chau and his attempt to reach the uncontacted Sentinelese tribe of North Sentinel Island. I will not go into detail, but for a list of reasons, I did not like the movie. But this blog is not about the movie; it’s about Chau and how Christians, I believe, should think about his venture to North Sentinel.

# I’m Not Mad, I’m Just Disappointed.

From the very first time I heard about Chau, it was clear to me that his story was a matter of significant controversy. It only takes a quick Google search to find a plethora of opinions from both believers and nonbelievers on Chau’s attempt to contact the Sentinelese people. The world, of course, looks at Chau’s actions and has no context for why anyone would do such a thing, so they think him a fool at best. However, some argue that he was far worse than a fool. I remember driving in my car, listening to a podcast in which the host celebrated John Chau’s death, calling him a radical colonialist. It was so sickening to me that I had to turn off the show after just a few short minutes.

Now, none of this should shock us as Christians because 1 Corinthians 1 tells us that the Gospel is foolishness to the world. Furthermore, Jesus reminds us in John 15:18 that, when we experience hatred from the world, we need to remember that the world hated him first. That said, the world’s reaction to Chau’s actions should not surprise Christians one bit. From their perspective, Chau risked endangering the Sentinelese with disease and sought to rob them of their “noble savagery” by imposing his Western worldview on them. To the world, Chau was at least an idiot, and possibly a wicked individual. That’s how the world sees the situation. But this is where I must admit, I am disappointed with the Church. Because there are some Christians who sound more like the world than the people of God on this issue. Let me explain.

When I watched the documentary, my reaction was, “Wow, this John guy was really bold and clearly loved the Lord.” And I honestly felt convicted to walk out my faith more boldly. But I quickly learned that not all Christians responded this way. Many professing believers labeled Chau reckless, overzealous, and even foolish. Now, let me be clear, I do not think John Chau did everything right. In fact, I probably would have taken a very different strategy in trying reach the tribe. But here’s the deal: he went. He obeyed Jesus’ Great Commission. And there are two responses that I think we as believers should have to Chau’s attempt to reach the Sentinelese.

# Let’s Not Just Offer Criticism; Let’s Offer Alternatives.

Much of my disappointment with how some members of the Church have handled Chau’s death comes from the fact that there have been a lot of critiques offered, but not very many alternatives. The truth is, we can criticize all we want, but Jesus Christ gave us a Great Commission, and that Great Commission includes taking the Gospel to _all_ the peoples — the Sentinelese included. No corner of the earth is exempt from God’s Great Commission to His Church. Someone has to go to North Sentinel Island. And that means that, rather than simply criticizing one man for his seemingly unsuccessful attempt at taking the Gospel there, we as the Church need to be thinking of possible methods of reaching the Sentinelese people with the Gospel of Jesus Christ. And the right place to start is on our knees, praying earnestly to God that he will send laborers into the harvest, even to North Sentinel Island.

# Let’s Be More Like Chau, Not Less.

This one might end up being more controversial, but I don’t know that anyone actually reads this blog, so I think it will all be alright. Here’s where I think Western Christians are missing it when it comes to John Allen Chau. First let me say that I am more than willing to agree that repeating Chau’s method is likely not the best way to reach the Sentinelese people. However, if I were to identify the primary issue with the modern Western church as it relates to God’s mission, it is not that we are too reckless. It is not that we are too zealous. It is not that we are too bold. It is that we are too _complacent_. We do not often find ourselves having to rein it in because we’re just too passionate about the spread of the Gospel. Instead, far more often we find ourselves struggling to even share the Gospel at all. So, when we see a man like Chau risking his life so that a totally unreached and unengaged people group on a remote island can have the chance to hear the Gospel for the first time, I think the right response for us, given our tendencies, is, “Lord, give us that kind of boldness. Would you make us willing to lay down our lives for your name’s sake, just as you willingly laid down your life for us.” That, I think, is the prayer Chau’s story should prompt us to lift up to the Lord.

# “Well done, good and faithful servant.”

Some say Chau died for nothing. But I think if the output of movies, articles, and podcasts has taught us anything, it is that he at least did not die for _nothing_. After all, we are still talking about him seven years later, and his story is prompting us to think more deeply about our own part in the Great Commission. So, at the very least, I do not think it is at all accurate to say that Chau died for nothing. In fact, as believers, we are obligated to take a completely different stance on Chau’s death. And that is because we do not view faithfulness in terms of results like the world does, but in terms of obedience. Chau could have died in obscurity with nothing earthly to show for his effort, and he still, I believe, would have heard from his Heavenly Father those precious words, “Well done, good and faithful servant.” I firmly believe that Chau’s death should serve as a wakeup call for the Church.

There are over 900 unreached and unengaged people groups (UUPGs) in South Asia alone, which accounts for about one-third of the world’s total number of UUPGs. By unreached and unengaged, we mean that there are no known believers in these communities, and they currently have no access to the good news about Jesus Christ. Over 42,000 South Asians die every day having never heard the Gospel. They will spend an eternity separated from God.

In Romans 10, Paul — under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit — writes, “How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how they are to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching? And how are they to preach unless they are sent? As it is written, ‘How beautiful are the feet of those who preach good news!’” (Romans 10:14-15). This is the reality. And there are right now many people who are facing eternity separated from God because they have rejected Him and have never heard and responded to the good news of Jesus Christ. My prayer is that the Lord will raise up more John Chau’s among us and that we will boldly take the Gospel to the forgotten and neglected peoples around the globe.
